    Section 5.3 The Natural Base

    It is difficult to explain to students before calculus why the natural base is important. This Lesson includes two interesting numerical facts about \(e\text{,}\) but we have not attempted to justify its relevance to exponential functions. If you wish, you might offer the two usual pictures to the class:
    🔗
    GOALS
    🔗
    • Evaluate and graph the natural exponential and natural log functions
      🔗
      🔗
    • Approximate the value of \(e\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Solve exponential equations base \(e\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Model exponential growth and decay with base \(e\)
      🔗

    Activity 5.3.2 Changing the Base.

    1. Because the number \(e\) is universal, it is useful to express any exponential growth or decay law with base \(e\text{.}\) This is easy to do.
      🔗
      First, notice that the laws of exponents tells that \(e^{kt} = (e^k)^t\text{.}\)
      🔗
      1. Follow the steps to write \(P=25(10)^t\) as \(P=25(e^k)^t\)
        🔗
        1. We need to replace \(10\) by \(e^k\text{.}\) That is, we need to find \(k\) so that \(e^k = 10\text{.}\) Solve this equation for \(k\text{.}\) Round your answer to tenths.
          🔗
          🔗
        2. Substitute your value for \(k\) into the formula for \(P\text{.}\)
          🔗
          🔗
        3. Check that the two formulas for \(P\) give the same outputs for \(x = 1,~x=2,~\) and \(x=3\text{.}\)
          🔗

          Aside
          2(b) It is very helpful to demonstrate that both forms of the function have the same graph.

          🔗
        🔗
        🔗
